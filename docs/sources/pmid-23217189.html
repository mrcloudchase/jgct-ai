Juvenile granulosa cell tumor of the testis: prenatal diagnosis and prescrotal approach.

Why it matters for JGCT

Rare neonatal testicular JGCT diagnosed prenatally, with markers, histology and orchiectomy-only outcome.

Key findings

  • A full-term newborn (3200 g) had a multicystic left testicular mass on fetal ultrasound at 36 weeks; postnatal ultrasound showed a 1.57 x 1.33 cm enlarged testis with a well-encapsulated complex cystic mass and increased Doppler flow; contralateral testis normal.
  • Karyotype, blood chemistry and abdominal ultrasound were normal; no ambiguous genitalia or dysmorphism; family history included two cases of Down syndrome.
  • Markers: AFP 6679 IU/mL, hCG 8 mIU/mL, testosterone 1.75 ng/mL, inhibin B 346 pg/mL; the authors note neonatal AFP is physiologically elevated and cite age-based reference ranges, but chose total orchiectomy because of the elevated AFP.
  • Left radical orchiectomy through a prescrotal/sub-inguinal incision; gonad pearly white, spongy yellow with many microcysts and no normal parenchyma; histology JGCT, inhibin and calretinin positive, AFP negative.
  • Ultrasound one month after surgery showed an empty left scrotum and no abdominal masses; the infant is healthy with annual ultrasound and clinical follow-up planned for at least 5 years.
  • The authors state, citing others, that JGCT is 1-5% of prepubertal testicular tumors, benign, hormonally inactive, and that only one prior prenatal diagnosis had been reported; they conclude that radical orchiectomy is sufficient and that follow-up is too short to exclude recurrence although none are reported in the literature.

Presentation

  • multicystic left testicular mass on fetal ultrasound at 36 weeks
  • increased testicular size at birth
  • painless, no torsion

Treatments reported

  • Left radical orchiectomy via sub-inguinal (prescrotal) incision; no chemotherapy or radiotherapy (neonatal testicular JGCT; n = 1)
    Outcome: uneventful; ultrasound at 1 month showed no pathological masses; healthy at report (short follow-up; annual checks for at least 5 years planned)

Notes

The title and abstract speak of a prescrotal approach, while the Conclusion argues that the only possible approach is inguinal and that a subinguinal approach exposes the cord; the paper uses the terms prescrotal and sub-inguinal interchangeably (internal inconsistency). The statement that the tumor is benign and that 'metastases have never been described' is the authors' citation-based claim. Follow-up in the report is only 1 month of documented data. The text says the report is 'healthy today' without a duration.

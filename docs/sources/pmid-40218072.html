Why it matters for JGCT

General pediatric ovarian mass review with only passing JGCT statements (markers, solid mass imaging, hormone production); useful as context for diagnostic work-up.

Key findings

  • Narrative review of ovarian cysts and masses from fetus to adolescence; JGCT is mentioned only briefly in three places and no JGCT patient data are given.
  • Sex cord-stromal tumors (thecomas, fibromas, juvenile granulosa cell tumor, Sertoli-Leydig cell tumors) are stated to be rare in children and adolescents, approximately 5% of ovarian malignancies; unlike germ cell and epithelial tumors they often present with hormone production (hirsutism, virilization, menstrual change, precocious puberty).
  • The review says a subset of sex cord-stromal tumors is associated with genetic aberrations and that an underlying cancer predisposition syndrome should always be considered.
  • Markers: inhibin A and B are listed as the marker for juvenile granulosa cell tumors; estradiol and testosterone are measured for hormonally active tumors; the absence of elevated markers does not exclude malignancy.
  • Imaging: masses with 50% or more solid component are considered malignant until histology shows otherwise, and solid ovarian masses in children can arise from sex cord-stromal tumors such as JGCT and Sertoli-Leydig cell tumors.
  • General management statements (not JGCT-specific): ovary-sparing minimally invasive surgery unless malignancy is strongly suspected; staging for suspected malignancy includes peritoneal lavage, biopsies and pelvic/periaortic lymph node sampling; follow-up every 3-6 months for 2-3 years then annually to 5 years or longer; treatment in specialized multidisciplinary centers.

Notes

JGCT-specific content is limited to a few sentences; no outcomes, molecular data or JGCT staging. Review figures are institutional images of non-JGCT lesions.

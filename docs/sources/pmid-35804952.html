Why it matters for JGCT

Imaging-focused review in which testicular JGCT occupies one short subsection; useful for epidemiology (1-5% of prepubertal testicular tumours), age at presentation, benign behaviour after testis-sparing surgery and a single illustrative case.

Key findings

  • Narrative imaging review of paediatric testicular and para-testicular tumours based on PubMed papers mainly 2010-2022; it reports that among non-germ-cell prepubertal testicular tumours, Leydig cell tumours (about 5%) are most frequent, followed by juvenile granulosa cell tumours.
  • Testicular juvenile granulosa cell tumour accounts for 1% to 5% of prepubertal testicular tumours (Table 1 gives under 5%) and usually presents in the first year of life, within the first 6 months; a few prenatal diagnoses have been reported.
  • Typical presentation is a painless testicular mass or enlarged testis, with possible hormonal changes including gynecomastia and anomalies of puberty.
  • There is no specific imaging appearance: tumours are classically well circumscribed with median size 1.5 cm, may contain cystic components or be entirely solid; macroscopically yellow-tan.
  • Histology: lobular architecture and follicular differentiation are characteristic; solid and reticular patterns are diagnostic pitfalls; immunohistochemistry helps distinguish from yolk sac tumours, which peak at a later age.
  • The illustrated case (Figure 7) was a 1-month-old boy with a well-circumscribed lobulated mass with a cystic component and calcifications, normal tumour markers, treated with partial orchidectomy by inguinal approach.
  • The review states the juvenile type is a benign entity with low recurrence after testis-sparing surgery, whereas adult testicular granulosa cell tumours have metastatic potential.
  • General management points: testis-sparing surgery with intra-operative frozen section for presumed benign lesions, with tumour size under 2.5 cm generally favourable; radical orchidectomy for malignant tumours.

Presentation

  • painless testicular mass or enlargement
  • possible gynecomastia and pubertal anomalies

Treatments reported

  • Testis-sparing surgery (partial orchidectomy) (testicular juvenile granulosa cell tumour, as described by the review; n = 1)
    Outcome: Review states juvenile type is benign with low recurrence after testis-sparing surgery; the illustrated 1-month-old had normal markers and partial inguinal orchidectomy

# What JGCT is and who gets it

Juvenile granulosa cell tumor (JGCT) is a rare tumor that grows from the granulosa cells, the cells in the ovary that surround the egg and make estrogen. It is one of the "sex cord-stromal" tumors. It mostly affects girls and young women, and a separate form occurs in the testicles of baby boys. It is a different disease from the adult-type granulosa cell tumor of older women, and the two are now treated as separate entities.

## Definition and how common it is

JGCT is a pure sex cord tumor made of primitive granulosa cells growing in solid and follicular patterns [pmid-37508611]. It makes up about 5% of all granulosa cell tumors; the adult type is the other 95% [pmid-38136408, pmid-23641362]. Among children and adolescents, though, it is the most common ovarian sex cord-stromal tumor: about 70% of such tumors before age 20 [pmid-32355841, pmid-37508611], about 75% in one imaging review [pmid-40723200], and the most common pediatric ovarian sex cord-stromal tumor in a consensus guidance [pmid-22525408]. In a Polish multicentre review of 595 girls operated on for ovarian lesions, JGCT was the single most common malignant diagnosis, 10 of 44 malignant tumors [pmid-35323325]. Population registries undercount it because ICD-O codes it as benign or of uncertain behavior, so higher-stage cases can be missed [pmid-27858560].

## Who gets it

- **Ovary.** The German STEP registry's 89 girls had a median age of 9.8 years (birth to 18), 44% under 8 [pmid-40272823]. The International Ovarian and Testicular Stromal Tumor (OTST) Registry's 70 patients had a median age of 10 years (1 day to 38), 93% under 20 [pmid-40267023]. The classic series put the mean at 13 years with 97% under 30 [pmid-38136408]. It also occurs in adults, including during pregnancy, and rarely after menopause [pmid-30547828, pmid-36992983, pmid-34688077]. See [JGCT in adults and in pregnancy](jgct-in-adults-and-pregnancy.md).
- **Testis.** Testicular JGCT is a tumor of infancy: in STEP, 24 boys had a median age of 16 days and all were diagnosed within the first year [pmid-40272823]. It behaves benignly, has never been reported to metastasize, and pathology consensus groups now call it "benign juvenile granulosa cell tumour" [pmid-32719989, pmid-40528632, pmid-42421431]. See [Testicular JGCT](testicular-jgct.md).

## How it differs from adult-type granulosa cell tumor

Adult-type tumors almost always carry the FOXL2 c.402C>G (p.C134W) mutation; JGCT essentially never does [pmid-23641362, pmid-38136408, pmid-27104151]. Adult-type tumors can recur ten or more years after treatment; JGCT relapses mostly within two years [pmid-42504512, pmid-36869369]. Under the microscope, JGCT lacks the grooved "coffee-bean" nuclei and Call-Exner bodies of the adult type and has more mitoses and larger, luteinized cells [pmid-23641362, pmid-38136408]. Mixed tumors with both patterns are described but rare [pmid-34688077].

## What is unknown

Why a tumor of young granulosa cells arises at all is not settled; the known drivers (AKT1 duplications, GNAS mutations) are present in a majority but not all tumors, and some sources still say no driver is established [pmid-26137586, pmid-39877787]. See [Genetics of JGCT](genetics-of-jgct.md). Whether ovarian and testicular JGCT share any biology beyond appearance is doubted: the testicular form lacks the ovarian mutations and shows monosomy 10 instead [pmid-42421431].

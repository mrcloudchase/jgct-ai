# JGCT in adults and in pregnancy

The name says juvenile, but about 3% of JGCTs are diagnosed after age 30, a handful after menopause, and a dozen or so during pregnancy. In adults the diagnosis is harder to make and to believe, and the management questions are different: how much to remove, and whether chemotherapy can wait for a baby.

## Adults

The classic series had 3% of patients over 30 and a range to 67 years (abstract only) [pmid-6465418, pmid-38136408]; a 13-case series had a median age of 17 with one postmenopausal patient, cystic JGCT averaged 22 years, and recent Italian and SEER series had median ages of 21.5 and 19.5 (abstracts only) [pmid-2910225, pmid-35353158, pmid-39561630, pmid-39863551]. Adult cases in this base include a 38-year-old whose 13 cm tumor grew within a year of normal-looking ovaries at a previous operation [pmid-30547828], a 40-year-old whose "cystadenoma" five years earlier was reinterpreted as JGCT when it recurred [pmid-32341591], a 43-year-old in a Czech molecular series who died of relapse [pmid-39773640], a 34-year-old transgender man with Ollier disease on testosterone [pmid-37840846], and gynandroblastomas with a JGCT component at 65 and 71 [pmid-32751726, pmid-30011982]. Adult-type tumors with minor juvenile foci are also described [pmid-34688077]. Some adult-age "JGCT" reports lack the immunohistochemistry or molecular data needed to be sure of the label, so caution is warranted [pmid-34688077]. Further adult cases known from abstracts only: a postmenopausal tumor [doi-10.51288_00840113], a woman in her 60s whose JGCT was taken for a Krukenberg metastasis of her gastric cancer [pmid-35944993], a 35-year-old's purely cystic tumor treated by cystectomy [doi-10.1186_s43055-025-01515-z], a 35-year-old with stage IV disease who relapsed at 9 months [pmid-17151531], and a 25-year-old whose high-mitotic-index tumor recurred within 6 months of BEP and was fatal [doi-10.1155_crog_7348622].

Many more adult cases carry the "juvenile" label, most in brief reports or abstracts with little supporting pathology, and they should be read with the same caution:

- **Read in full.** A Japanese 25-year-old with a 17 cm tumor, 51 mitoses per 10 fields, ascites and pleural effusion, who died about three months after surgery with a supraclavicular node metastasis [doi-10.5795_jjscc.31.74]; a virilized 35-year-old in Serbia with a 3 cm tumor found at hysterectomy, the juvenile label resting on one sentence of histology [doi-10.1515_sjecr-2017-0016]; a Turkish 23-year-old with recurrence within two months whose inhibin- and calretinin-negative tumor may not be JGCT at all [doi-10.5505_aot.2012.33042]; four Moroccan patients aged 18 to 31 [doi-10.21474_ijar01_14578]; and a Japanese woman whose tumor at 12 was called juvenile and whose FOXL2-negative tumor at 40 was judged adult-type, a case the authors cannot resolve between a new tumor and a 28-year recurrence [doi-10.57291_jsgo.44.2_161].
- **Abstracts only.** German women of 23 and 25 with 5 liters or more of ascites but no peritoneal spread [doi-10.1055_s-2006-952364, doi-10.1055_s-0036-1592834]; a 28-year-old with stage pT3b disease and no FOXL2 or DICER1 mutation [doi-10.1055_s-0044-1790967]; a 19-year-old in Tunis [doi-10.1002_uog.24062]; two Spanish women under 30 [doi-10.1016_j.gamo.2016.03.006]; a 36-year-old with recurrence and no other detail [doi-10.4321_s0378-48352006000700004]; and six Chinese JGCT with a mean age of 35.3 years [pmid-23898526].
- **Self-report.** In a survey of a granulosa cell tumor support group, of the 52 respondents reporting the juvenile type, 23.1% said they were diagnosed at 30-39, 9.6% at 40-49 and 1.9% at 50-59, all premenopausal; the diagnoses were not verified [pmid-37011030].

The opposite error also occurs: a pregnant 39-year-old's tumor was twice reported as high-grade JGCT and proved to be small cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type [pmid-42463449]. See [Pathology](pathology-and-differential-diagnosis.md). Among adults, surgery has often been hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy when childbearing is complete [pmid-30547828, pmid-37840846], though fertility-sparing surgery in a 33-year-old with stage IIIB disease was followed by more than 5 years without recurrence [pmid-36992983]. 

Recurrent JGCT in adults carries a worse outlook than recurrent adult-type tumor: median survival after recurrence 32 versus 182 months in one registry [pmid-40815979].

## Pregnancy

A 2026 report counts 12 published JGCTs diagnosed in pregnancy over three decades, stages IA to IIIB [pmid-41797949]. Two cases are recorded here in full: a 33-year-old whose ruptured 15 cm tumor with bowel metastases (stage IIIB) was resected at 25 weeks, with BEP delayed until six weeks after a 37-week delivery and no recurrence at more than 5 years [pmid-36992983]; and a 28-year-old operated at 23 weeks for a stage IA tumor, delivered by cesarean at 37 weeks with completion staging, no chemotherapy and no recurrence at 24 months [pmid-41797949]. Points the authors make: inhibin B is a usable marker in pregnancy because pregnancy raises inhibin A but not B [pmid-41797949, pmid-36992983]; large adnexal masses are best removed in the second trimester; chemotherapy is avoided in the first trimester and within three weeks of delivery, and whether it can be delayed until after birth is a shared decision [pmid-36992983, pmid-41797949]. One patient conceived six months after surgery for a stage IA tumor that had presented with galactorrhea [pmid-27047664]. Pregnancy cases known from abstracts only: two JGCTs with trisomy 12 treated conservatively during pregnancy with normal deliveries [pmid-10509309]; a 22-year-old [pmid-9289669]; a rapidly fatal case [pmid-8051349]; a 1157 g tumor causing torsion at 22 weeks, removed with the pregnancy preserved [doi-10.1089_gyn.2016.0073]; a large tumor found at 33 weeks with suspected torsion at 35 [pmid-22226215]; a stage IV presentation [doi-10.18535_ijmsci_v13i.07.02]; and an enormous tumor appearing within a year of delivery [doi-10.26420_austingynecolcaserep.2023.1036]. Two more are known from abstracts: a 35-year-old with twins whose 17 cm tumor was found at 33 weeks and removed at a repeat cesarean at 34 weeks (stage pT1a; she declined completion surgery), where the authors say pregnancy-related ultrasound changes delayed suspicion [doi-10.1055_s-0043-1769866]; and a Spanish woman under 30 who delivered a healthy baby a year into follow-up [doi-10.1016_j.gamo.2016.03.006]. Counts of published pregnancy cases differ: a 2025 Moroccan thesis summary reporting two cases states that fewer than 10 had been published (abstract only) [doi-10.83129_toubkal-29149], against the 12 tabulated in the 2026 report [pmid-41797949]. Hyperreactio luteinalis of pregnancy is the benign mimic to know (abstract only) [pmid-33323862].

## Hormones and JGCT in adults

Testosterone therapy in a transgender man with a 3.3 kg androgen-receptor-positive JGCT raised the question of whether exogenous androgens fed the tumor; his doctors stopped testosterone [pmid-37840846]. An androgen-secreting recurrent JGCT in a 17-year-old coincided with gender dysphoria, and the authors suggest the tumor's testosterone may have contributed [pmid-33362514]. Whether treatment for polycystic ovary disease (metformin, combined pill) contributed to a 30 cm JGCT 18 months later is a hypothesis the reporting authors raise [pmid-24596770]. None of these are more than single observations.

## What is unknown

Whether adult-onset JGCT is the same disease as the childhood tumor, since few adult cases are sequenced [pmid-34688077]; the safety of delaying chemotherapy in pregnancy beyond the two reported cases [pmid-36992983]; and whether hormone treatments of any kind affect risk or recurrence [pmid-37840846, pmid-36980387].

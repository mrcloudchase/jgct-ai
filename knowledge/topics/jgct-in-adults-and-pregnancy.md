# JGCT in adults and in pregnancy

The name says juvenile, but about 3% of JGCTs are diagnosed after age 30, a handful after menopause, and a dozen or so during pregnancy. In adults the diagnosis is harder to make and to believe, and the management questions are different: how much to remove, and whether chemotherapy can wait for a baby.

## Adults

The classic series had 3% of patients over 30 and a range to 67 years (abstract only) [pmid-6465418, pmid-38136408]; a 13-case series had a median age of 17 with one postmenopausal patient, cystic JGCT averaged 22 years, and recent Italian and SEER series had median ages of 21.5 and 19.5 (abstracts only) [pmid-2910225, pmid-35353158, pmid-39561630, pmid-39863551]. Adult cases in this base include a 38-year-old whose 13 cm tumor grew within a year of normal-looking ovaries at a previous operation [pmid-30547828], a 40-year-old whose "cystadenoma" five years earlier was reinterpreted as JGCT when it recurred [pmid-32341591], a 43-year-old in a Czech molecular series who died of relapse [pmid-39773640], a 34-year-old transgender man with Ollier disease on testosterone [pmid-37840846], and gynandroblastomas with a JGCT component at 65 and 71 [pmid-32751726, pmid-30011982]. Adult-type tumors with minor juvenile foci are also described [pmid-34688077]. Some adult-age "JGCT" reports lack the immunohistochemistry or molecular data needed to be sure of the label, so caution is warranted [pmid-34688077]. Further adult cases known from abstracts only: a postmenopausal tumor [doi-10.51288_00840113], a woman in her 60s whose JGCT was taken for a Krukenberg metastasis of her gastric cancer [pmid-35944993], a 35-year-old's purely cystic tumor treated by cystectomy [doi-10.1186_s43055-025-01515-z], a 35-year-old with stage IV disease who relapsed at 9 months [pmid-17151531], and a 25-year-old whose high-mitotic-index tumor recurred within 6 months of BEP and was fatal [doi-10.1155_crog_7348622]. Among adults, surgery has often been hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy when childbearing is complete [pmid-30547828, pmid-37840846], though fertility-sparing surgery in a 33-year-old with stage IIIB disease was followed by more than 5 years without recurrence [pmid-36992983]. Recurrent JGCT in adults carries a worse outlook than recurrent adult-type tumor: median survival after recurrence 32 versus 182 months in one registry [pmid-40815979].

## Pregnancy

A 2026 report counts 12 published JGCTs diagnosed in pregnancy over three decades, stages IA to IIIB [pmid-41797949]. Two cases are recorded here in full: a 33-year-old whose ruptured 15 cm tumor with bowel metastases (stage IIIB) was resected at 25 weeks, with BEP delayed until six weeks after a 37-week delivery and no recurrence at more than 5 years [pmid-36992983]; and a 28-year-old operated at 23 weeks for a stage IA tumor, delivered by cesarean at 37 weeks with completion staging, no chemotherapy and no recurrence at 24 months [pmid-41797949]. Points the authors make: inhibin B is a usable marker in pregnancy because pregnancy raises inhibin A but not B [pmid-41797949, pmid-36992983]; large adnexal masses are best removed in the second trimester; chemotherapy is avoided in the first trimester and within three weeks of delivery, and whether it can be delayed until after birth is a shared decision [pmid-36992983, pmid-41797949]. One patient conceived six months after surgery for a stage IA tumor that had presented with galactorrhea [pmid-27047664]. Pregnancy cases known from abstracts only: two JGCTs with trisomy 12 treated conservatively during pregnancy with normal deliveries [pmid-10509309]; a 22-year-old [pmid-9289669]; a rapidly fatal case [pmid-8051349]; a 1157 g tumor causing torsion at 22 weeks, removed with the pregnancy preserved [doi-10.1089_gyn.2016.0073]; a large tumor found at 33 weeks with suspected torsion at 35 [pmid-22226215]; a stage IV presentation [doi-10.18535_ijmsci_v13i.07.02]; and an enormous tumor appearing within a year of delivery [doi-10.26420_austingynecolcaserep.2023.1036]. Hyperreactio luteinalis of pregnancy is the benign mimic to know (abstract only) [pmid-33323862].

## Hormones and JGCT in adults

Testosterone therapy in a transgender man with a 3.3 kg androgen-receptor-positive JGCT raised the question of whether exogenous androgens fed the tumor; his doctors stopped testosterone [pmid-37840846]. An androgen-secreting recurrent JGCT in a 17-year-old coincided with gender dysphoria, and the authors suggest the tumor's testosterone may have contributed [pmid-33362514]. Whether treatment for polycystic ovary disease (metformin, combined pill) contributed to a 30 cm JGCT 18 months later is a hypothesis the reporting authors raise [pmid-24596770]. None of these are more than single observations.

## What is unknown

Whether adult-onset JGCT is the same disease as the childhood tumor, since few adult cases are sequenced [pmid-34688077]; the safety of delaying chemotherapy in pregnancy beyond the two reported cases [pmid-36992983]; and whether hormone treatments of any kind affect risk or recurrence [pmid-37840846, pmid-36980387].
